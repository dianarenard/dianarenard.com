Reading people

Trained as an engineer at Siberian State Aerospace University and rebuilt through drawing when her life came apart, Diana Renard has founded three companies in under two years: Mindologue for the mind, NAPKIN for the first date and Regime for the body.

A profile, October 2026

Halfway through a first date, Diana Renard slid a paper napkin across the table and asked the man opposite to draw something. He was handsome, he made her laugh, and she was falling fast. He laughed too, and drew.

She knew what drawings give away. She had built an app on it. That night she ran his napkin through Mindologue, and the reading did not flatter him: a heart that watches from a distance, defenses everywhere. She read it twice and chose him anyway. "Red is my favorite color," she writes, "so I ignored every red flag."

Everything the drawing showed came true. "It took me a whole relationship to see what one drawing showed me in a minute," she writes. The napkin became NAPKIN, a first-date app coming soon to iPhone, and the third company Renard has founded in under two years. To understand why she trusted a drawing in the first place, you have to start in Siberia.

A systems mind

In August 2013, Renard enrolled at Siberian State Aerospace University in Krasnoyarsk to study computer science. Four years there taught her to see problems as systems: inputs, outputs, feedback, and the one wrong assumption that breaks everything downstream.

When she turned to the human mind, she brought that training with her. She went on to study general psychology and to specialize in art therapy, and she describes her work as sitting "at the intersection of projective psychology, systemic logic, and Stoic philosophy." Psychology supplies the questions. Stoicism supplies the posture: attend to what you can actually control. Engineering supplies the habit of measuring before you prescribe.

When it came apart

The turn from systems to selves was personal. In the description of her workbook, Unfolding Your True Self, Renard writes about the period that changed her direction: "When the weight of broken relationships, work struggles, and financial pressures pressed down on me, drawing became my method to process emotions and understand my fears."

What she found has a long clinical lineage. Projective psychology, the tradition that runs from Hermann Rorschach's inkblots in 1921 to the House-Tree-Person drawing test of 1948, rests on a simple observation: people reveal in images what they cannot yet say in words. "Most people struggle to put their deepest blocks into words," Renard writes, "so I teach them to use symbols, lines, and colors instead."

The exercises became a 30-day workbook, published in March 2026. The workbook became an app. Mindologue, which she co-founded in January 2025 with Fabrice Colozzi, lets you photograph a drawing and receive a reflection on what it may be expressing, alongside journaling and guided meditation. In August, at AI Salon Miami, she demonstrated it live to a room of founders and researchers, at an evening whose program asked a question that could have been her thesis: can a drawing predict your psychology?

The napkin

Her own first date was the hardest test of that question, and NAPKIN turns it into a ritual anyone can use. The app gives you the line, so asking feels like flirting. Your date agrees to play and draws love on a napkin in one minute. On the way home, NAPKIN shows how they lean in love and what to watch next time, and after a few dates it gives a straight answer: continue, slow down or walk away.

The idea rests on a line from NAPKIN's own website: "Anyone can rehearse an answer. Nobody rehearses a drawing." The rules are hers too. A date says yes before anything is read, and the app never reads a drawing in secret.

"I can't get that time back. You don't have to lose yours."Diana Renard, NAPKIN founder's letter

From the mind to the body

If Mindologue is about the parts of ourselves we cannot put into words, Regime, founded in April 2026, is about the parts of women's health that research never put into numbers.

In January 2013, months before Renard enrolled in Krasnoyarsk, the United States Food and Drug Administration told doctors to cut the dose of zolpidem, the sleeping pill best known as Ambien, in half, for women only. It lingers longer in women's bodies, and it had been on pharmacy shelves for two decades. From 1977 to 1993, federal guidance kept women who could become pregnant out of the earliest drug trials. A 2001 government review found that eight of ten prescription drugs recently withdrawn from the American market posed greater health risks for women. In a 2021 review of more than 5,000 sport and exercise science studies, just six percent looked at women alone.

Renard sees the cost in ordinary weeks. A woman's energy, hunger, sleep, focus and recovery move with her hormones all month long, while nearly every plan she is handed assumes they stay still. Regime reads her cycle, food, sleep and blood tests and turns them into a plan for the day she is actually having. Each morning starts with a sixty-second check-in; the next day the app asks "did it help?" and learns what works for that woman rather than for an average one.

"Most health plans are written for a body that stays the same every week. A woman's body doesn't."Diana Renard, Regime founder's letter

How she builds

Renard designs and builds Regime and NAPKIN herself, and she writes rules into them that most apps skip. In Regime, artificial intelligence may reword a message but never add a number or a medical claim, and every guide lists its sources. In NAPKIN, the people you date never see your notes or verdicts. Regime never sells data. Her Regime letter ends with an email address and four words: "I read every message."

The answer on the napkin

The drawing from that first date now opens NAPKIN's website: a heart with its eyes on stalks, watching from a safe distance, a grin with every tooth on show, and "I love you" written just outside it. Renard put it there for every woman who arrives with a charming stranger on her mind. "I had the answer on a napkin," she writes. "I didn't listen."
